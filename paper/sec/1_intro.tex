\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

End-to-end driving models learn to map sensor inputs to a trajectory by imitating
large corpora of expert driving. The training objective rewards agreement with
the logged future, which pushes the policy toward a single consensus behavior. In
deployment this acts as one average driver: the same driving style is produced for
every user and, within a scenario type, for every situation. This is usually read
as a personalization limitation, but it has two consequences that can be measured
directly.

The first concerns what safety scores can and cannot see. On the same scenarios,
planners that reach comparable closed-loop safety can sit far apart on a
multi-dimensional behavioral profile of speed, acceleration, jerk, headway, and
spacing (\cref{fig:teaser}). A safety-only objective therefore does not constrain
driving style, and a single safety number does not reveal where a policy sits on
that profile. The second concerns multi-modality. When several maneuvers are
equally reasonable, a model trained to match an averaged target and decoded as a
single mode tends to produce a trajectory that lies between the modes rather than
committing to one. Driving style is the quantity this paradigm removes. It is not
a coarse label but a measurable and interpretable description of how a vehicle is
driven, which should follow user preference while still respecting the scene.

Prior work on personalized or stylized driving usually conditions imitation on a
driver profile or a discrete style label, and often reports that emphasizing
style lowers closed-loop scores. We take a different view. Much of the reported
tension follows from safety metrics that do not separate behaviors a user would
consider different, and from style being entangled with scene understanding
inside a single-mode decoder. What is missing is a formulation in which style is
an explicit and controllable axis, measured on its own terms and produced by a
decoder that can commit to a mode.

We build such a formulation on a vision-language-action (VLA) backbone and call it
YouDrive. Our starting point is a hypothesis about representation: a driving style
behaves like a task vector, a reusable direction that can be added to the base
policy, scaled, and combined. We distill each persona into a low-rank adapter over
the frozen backbone, treat its weight increment as that direction, and apply it in
the VLA content space through one interpolation coefficient. We use a VLA rather
than a geometric encoder on purpose: task-vector control has been observed in
language-grounded representations, where directions correspond to interpretable
behavior, whereas a geometric feature space is organized around scene geometry and
is a poor space in which to define a behavioral direction. To keep style
expressible at decode time, we replace the discrete action codebook of the
backbone with a flow-matching decoder that samples several feasible candidates and
returns one, so that averaging does not erase the behavior a style is meant to
express. Finally, we measure style with YDMS, a metric that gates hard safety and
task requirements and then scores interpretable, scenario-normalized style
dimensions.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A driving-style metric, YDMS, that separates hard safety and task gates
    from interpretable style dimensions, together with an analysis of what these
    dimensions do and do not capture on NAVSIM. We regard the metric as the most
    direct attempt to make the average-driver problem measurable, and we are
    explicit that the current version is preliminary and improvable.
    \item A flow-matching trajectory decoder for a VLA backbone, controlled by
    style task vectors. The decoder is an architectural change from the discrete
    codebook to a continuous, sampling planner, and the task-vector control is a
    methodological one that exposes style as a single, composable axis in the VLA
    content space.
    \item An empirical study on NAVSIM that spans a wide, controllable style range
    at strong safety (up to 0.954 PDMS), including per-teacher and blended
    personas and an ensemble-decoder variant of the same personalization idea,
    along with ablations on the conditioning space and the personalization
    mechanism.
\end{itemize}
