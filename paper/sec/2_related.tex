\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{End-to-end and VLA planning.}
End-to-end planners on nuPlan and NAVSIM map sensor inputs to a trajectory without
a hand-built intermediate stack~\cite{transfuser,navsim}. A recent line replaces the
perception-planning pipeline with vision-language-action backbones that reason over
multi-view images, ego state, and a navigation command~\cite{autovla,qwenvl}.
AutoVLA~\cite{autovla} decodes trajectories by selecting from a discrete action
codebook. We keep the VLA as a scene encoder and replace the codebook with a
continuous flow-matching decoder, which lets us both sample several maneuvers and
control style in the content space.

\paragraph{Personalized and stylized driving.}
Work on personalized or stylized driving conditions imitation on a driver profile or
a style label, and often reports that closed-loop scores drop when style is
emphasized. A common first step, taken by recent style-aware benchmarks such as
StyleDrive~\cite{styledrive}, is to discretize style into a few categories such as
aggressive, normal, and conservative. Such labels are a useful starting point, but
they tend to correlate with safety margins while separating weakly on measured
kinematics, and a small, imbalanced label set carries limited information about how a
vehicle is actually driven. We read the reported style-safety tension mainly as a
consequence of two things: safety metrics that do not distinguish behaviors users
would treat as different, and style being entangled with scene understanding inside a
single-mode decoder. We therefore represent style as a continuous, multi-dimensional,
scenario-normalized axis and provide a metric that reports where a policy sits on it.

\paragraph{Generative planning.}
DiffusionDrive~\cite{diffusiondrive} and GoalFlow~\cite{goalflow} generate several
trajectory candidates and then select one. These planners condition on geometric
bird's-eye or image-encoder features. We build on rectified flow~\cite{rectflow} and
flow matching~\cite{flowmatching} with a conditioned DiT/AdaLN
decoder~\cite{dit}, and we decode in a kinematically constrained control space for
dynamic feasibility. Our main departure is the conditioning signal: the generator is
driven by dense, language-grounded VLA content rather than a pooled perception
feature, which is also the space in which we define style directions
(\cref{sec:method:overview}).

\paragraph{Trajectory scoring and selection.}
Sample-and-select planners need a scorer. PDM-Closed~\cite{pdmclosed} scores
proposals with a lightweight simulator, and learned scorers such as
Hydra-MDP~\cite{hydramdp} and generalized trajectory scoring~\cite{gtrs,wote} distill
simulation signals into a network. We adopt the sample-and-select view; because style
is a property of the sampled set, our style results do not depend on which of these
scorers is used.

\paragraph{Style directions and adapters.}
Representing a behavior as a direction in a model's parameter space connects to
LoRA~\cite{lora} and to task arithmetic~\cite{taskvector}, where task vectors are
weight increments that can be added, scaled, and combined. We distill each persona
into such a low-rank increment and expose it as an interpolatable direction, so that a
single coefficient and linear blends control style without retraining the backbone.

\paragraph{Closed-loop metrics.}
NAVSIM PDMS~\cite{navsim,pdmclosed} aggregates no-at-fault collision, drivable-area
and direction compliance, ego progress, time-to-collision, and comfort; the extended
EPDMS suite adds lane-keeping, extended comfort, and reactive agents. These scores
capture safety and task completion well, but by construction they are largely
invariant to the stylistic differences a user cares about, which is one of the gaps
our metric is meant to fill.
