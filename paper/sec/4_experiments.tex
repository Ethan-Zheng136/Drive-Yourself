\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}
\paragraph{Backbone and training.}
Our planner uses the Qwen2.5-VL-3B~\cite{qwenvl} vision-language model as its scene
encoder. Multi-view camera images, the ego status, and the navigation command are
tokenized and encoded into a sequence of content tokens that summarize both the scene
and the requested maneuver. This encoder and its warm-start follow
AutoVLA~\cite{autovla}, but the trajectory pathway is different. AutoVLA decodes a
trajectory autoregressively by emitting tokens from a 2048-entry action codebook (real
per-step footprint transitions clustered offline), which fixes a single mode before the
trajectory is formed; we remove the codebook and instead condition the flow-matching
decoder of \cref{sec:method:fm} on the content tokens through cross-attention. That
decoder flows in a control space of longitudinal acceleration and yaw rate under a
kinematic unicycle model, is integrated with $K$ steps, and is sampled $N{=}16$ times
per frame. We initialize from the released \texttt{AutoVLA\_PDMS\_89} checkpoint and
fine-tune the language backbone jointly with the decoder on NAVSIM navtrain, with the
vision encoder frozen and the human trajectory as the target. The resulting model, not
the original codebook policy, is what we call ``base'' ($\alpha{=}0$). Each persona
adapter is distilled from a teacher planner:
\texttt{gtrs}~\cite{gtrs}, \texttt{hydra\_mdp}~\cite{hydramdp}, \texttt{wote}~\cite{wote},
\texttt{transfuser}~\cite{transfuser}, \texttt{goalflow}~\cite{goalflow}, and
\texttt{ddv2}~\cite{diffusiondrive}, and is applied through \cref{eq:interp}. We
evaluate on the NAVSIM~\cite{navsim} navtest split (about 12k scenarios, 8 shards)
and report scenario-weighted PDMS (v1) and, for the extended suite, EPDMS (v2).
Alongside safety we report the YDMS style axes, Dynamics (DAI), Proximity (PAI), and
Social (SAI) alignment, each normalized so that the human driver sits at 50; the full
set of axes and the raw kinematics are defined in \cref{sec:suppl:v1full}.

\subsection{Style and safety on navtest (v1)}
\label{sec:exp:v1}
\Cref{tab:v1} reports PDMS on navtest together with the YDMS style axes, for the base
policy, the six persona teachers, and three persona blends. To keep the comparison on
a single operating point, every persona is applied at full strength ($\alpha{=}1$);
the continuous behaviour as $\alpha$ varies is the subject of \cref{sec:exp:style}. At
full strength the style axes spread over a wide range, from an aggressive persona
(\texttt{ddv2}, PAI~39) to a cautious one (\texttt{gtrs}, PAI~65), while PDMS stays
within about one point of the base and improves for the dynamic personas and for every
blend; the strongest configuration, a \texttt{ddv2}/\texttt{gtrs} blend, reaches
0.954. The six teachers settle in distinct regions of the style axes, and the blends
reach points that no single teacher attains, which is the behavior expected if
personas act as composable directions. The set of teachers is not fixed: adding a
persona only requires distilling one adapter, without retraining the backbone.

\subsection{Style is a systematic per-driver signal}
\label{sec:exp:systematic}
A style axis is only meaningful if style is a real, per-driver signal rather than
scene noise. On a fixed set of tokens we collect per-driver trajectories for 16
drivers (the human, external planners, and our base) and run a one-way variance
decomposition of per-token kinematics with driver identity as the factor
(\cref{tab:vardecomp}). Driver identity explains a mean $\eta^2$ of 0.27, up to 0.46
for lateral jerk, with F-statistics above one thousand, so how a driver accelerates,
brakes, and jerks is strongly driver-specific. The one exception is average speed
($\eta^2{=}0.005$), which is set by the road and traffic; this is why average speed
is a weak style coordinate on navtest. A coarse aggressive/normal/conservative label
separates the same kinematics with $\eta^2 \le 0.09$~\cite{styledrive}, about four to
five times weaker than per-driver identity. This supports treating style as a
continuous, per-driver axis, and it is also part of why we regard the current YDMS as
preliminary: some of its dimensions separate weakly and are reported for
completeness rather than as discriminative signal.

\subsection{Style controllability and multi-modal commitment}
\label{sec:exp:style}
Sweeping $\alpha$ traces a continuous path through style space: the axes and the
underlying kinematics move from the base point toward each teacher, so a single
coefficient spans a range rather than switching between presets
(\cref{tab:following}). For the \texttt{ddv2} persona, raising $\alpha$ from 0 to 1
drives Proximity from 56.5 down to 38.9 (closer following) and Dynamics to saturation
near 100, while the $L_2$ distance to the teacher trajectory falls from 18.0 to under
9; PDMS rises from the base 0.912 to a peak of 0.947 near $\alpha{=}0.75$ and then
plateaus, so the persona is expressed strongly without giving up safety. Multi-modality
is common enough to matter on navtest. Among
scenes with sufficient driver coverage, about 7.4\% are laterally bimodal (left or
right) and about 13.9\% are longitudinally bimodal (brake or go). In such scenes a
single-mode regressor tends to return a path between the modes, whereas the sampling
decoder draws distinct maneuvers and returns one, which is the mechanism that keeps a
style expressible where averaging would remove it.

\subsection{Do personas behave as task vectors?}
\label{sec:exp:vector}
The task-vector view of \cref{sec:method:style} predicts three properties, which we
test directly. Linearity: as $\alpha$ increases the style axes should move
monotonically and close to linearly toward the teacher; we fit this on the full
$\alpha$ sweep and report the fit. Composition: a blend $\sum_i w_i \Delta_i$ should
match the weighted sum of the single-persona style shifts; we compare the measured
blend to that prediction. Negation: applying $-\alpha$ should move style in the
opposite direction, for example from an aggressive persona toward a conservative one.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:exp:ablate}
Following the component-wise style of PrismAD~\cite{prismad}, we isolate the parts of
the method that the claims rest on. The conditioning ablation replaces VLA content
with a geometric image or bird's-eye feature of matched capacity while holding the
decoder fixed, testing whether the density claim of \cref{sec:method:overview} holds
(\cref{tab:ablate_cond}). The personalization ablation compares the task-vector
interpolation used above with an ensemble decoder, in which several flow-matching
heads are trained jointly and a per-persona adapter selects among them at inference;
this is a second realization of the same personalization idea and lets us ask which
mechanism gives better style coverage at equal safety (\cref{tab:ablate_mech}). We
also ablate the decoder itself against the discrete codebook of the backbone, and
sweep the number of candidates $N$ and integration steps $K$.
